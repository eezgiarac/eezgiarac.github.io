# Unit 4: Comparison of Waterfall and Agile Methodologies

## Introduction

Waterfall follows a planned sequence of development stages, whereas Agile uses short, repeated cycles. Neither approach suits every project. Their value depends on requirement stability, regulation, risk and stakeholder feedback.

## Flexibility and Changing Requirements

Waterfall completes requirements, design, implementation, testing and deployment in order. This creates clear milestones, documentation and cost estimates. However, a change discovered during testing may require earlier work to be revised, increasing cost and delay. Royce (1970), whose work is associated with Waterfall, warned that a simple sequential process creates risk and proposed feedback between stages. His historically important paper examined large technical systems, so it is not universal evidence for modern projects.

Agile offers flexibility because users can review each working increment and suggest changes for the next cycle. This reflects the Agile Manifesto’s emphasis on collaboration and responding to change (Beck et al., 2001). However, the manifesto presents practitioner principles rather than empirical proof. Agile may also create changing scope, weak documentation or rework when priorities are unclear.

## Project Size and Suitability

Waterfall may suit a project with stable requirements, fixed contracts or formal approval stages. For example, the safety-control component of an EV charger may require detailed specifications, traceability and documented testing. These controls can also help coordinate a large project, although project size alone does not make Waterfall the correct choice.

Agile is effective for collaborative teams developing products whose requirements may evolve. An EV charging application could release journey planning first and add payment or accessibility improvements after feedback. Large organisations can also use Agile, but several teams require additional coordination. Therefore, Agile is not limited to small projects.

## Risk Management and Quality Assurance

Waterfall manages risk through early planning, formal reviews and documented test stages. This supports accountability and regulatory evidence. Its limitation is that users may not see working software until late in the project, so usability problems or misunderstood requirements can remain hidden.

Agile reduces this exposure through frequent demonstrations, testing and feedback. Dybå and Dingsøyr’s (2008) systematic review reported benefits such as collaboration and customer satisfaction, but also found that the available empirical evidence had methodological weaknesses. Similarly, Serrador and Pinto (2015) identified an association between Agile practices and project success; however, association does not prove that Agile alone caused the results. Quality still depends on skilled teams, automated testing and disciplined technical practices.

## Recommendation

Agile is the stronger choice for customer-facing software with uncertain or changing requirements. Waterfall is more appropriate when requirements are stable and formal evidence must be approved at fixed stages. A hybrid approach is most suitable for a complex EV charging project: hardware and safety certification can follow controlled milestones, while the mobile application can be improved iteratively. This recommendation responds to the different risk profiles of the project rather than assuming that one methodology is always superior.

**Topic:** Artificial Intelligence on Instagram: Creativity or a Crisis of Trust?

**Status:** Completed

This collaborative discussion explored how artificial intelligence is changing social media, with Instagram as the main example. My initial post examined AI-powered recommendation systems, personalised content, opportunities for small businesses, misinformation and the potential effects of algorithmic personalisation on society.

Peer feedback introduced an additional perspective on independent content creators. Generative AI can reduce production costs and make content creation more accessible, but it may also reproduce stereotypes and encourage similar or repetitive content.

In my final summary, I connected these observations with wider concerns about deepfake-enabled social engineering, automation bias and the importance of human oversight. The discussion highlighted that responsible AI adoption requires transparency, accountability, independent verification and effective platform governance.
