## Artificial Intelligence on Instagram: Creativity or a Crisis of Trust?

## 1. Initial Discussion Post

When I first started using Instagram, most posts appeared to represent people's daily lives and genuine experiences. Today, however, it is increasingly difficult to determine whether an image, video or even a person shown on the platform is real. One major reason for this change is the growing use of artificial intelligence (AI) within the social media industry.

Instagram uses AI-powered recommender systems to analyse user behaviour and decide which posts appear in the Explore page and Reels feed. This algorithmic personalisation helps users discover relevant content while allowing businesses, particularly smaller companies, to reach specific audiences more efficiently. Saheb, Sidaoui and Schmarzo (2024) argue that the convergence of AI and social media creates valuable opportunities for personalisation, marketing and user engagement.

Nevertheless, personalisation does not always benefit users. Algorithms designed to maximise engagement may repeatedly present similar content instead of exposing individuals to diverse perspectives. Lorenz-Spreen et al. (2023) found that digital media can improve access to information and encourage participation, but it may also contribute to political polarisation and declining institutional trust. This demonstrates that the same technology can produce both commercial benefits and negative societal consequences.

Generative AI has made this challenge more complex because realistic but false images and videos can now be produced quickly and at a low cost. Consequently, misinformation may become more persuasive and difficult to identify (Raman et al., 2024). As a recent example, Meta announced in 2024 that it would label AI-generated images posted on Instagram, Facebook and Threads (Meta, 2024). Although this represents an important step towards transparency, its effectiveness is limited because not every AI-generated item contains detectable technical markers.

In my view, AI has made Instagram more creative, personalised and commercially accessible. However, its benefits should be supported by transparent labelling, accountable algorithms, human oversight and improved digital literacy to protect user trust.

## 3. Final Summary Post

**Submitted:** 21 August 2026

The discussion demonstrated that artificial intelligence has transformed social media through personalized recommendations, targeted advertising and generative content. The initial analysis identified a fundamental tension: AI can improve user engagement and business efficiency while increasing misinformation, ideological polarization and exposure to biased content.

Peer feedback extended this analysis by introducing the perspective of content creators. Generative AI can reduce production costs and enable independent creators to produce professional content without extensive equipment or large teams (Lyu et al., 2024). Nevertheless, greater accessibility does not necessarily produce greater creativity. AI-generated content is developed from patterns within existing training data and may consequently reproduce stereotypes or prioritize conventional representations (Gao, Xia and Zhong, 2025). Platform governance must therefore address not only misinformation but also content diversity and algorithmic bias.

The peer discussions also revealed similar challenges in cybersecurity and healthcare. Deepfake-enabled social engineering demonstrates that technical detection cannot offer complete protection because synthetic-media generation and detection continue to evolve simultaneously (Zia et al., 2024). Effective organisational protection therefore requires layered controls, including independent verification, separate communication channels and dual approval for high-value transactions. Similarly, automation bias in healthcare illustrates the danger of accepting persuasive AI-generated recommendations without sufficient professional evaluation (World Health Organization, 2024).

Overall, the collaborative discussion developed the initial understanding of AI from a simple comparison of benefits and risks into a broader evaluation of technology, human behavior and governance. Although social media, cybersecurity and healthcare represent different contexts, each demonstrates that technical accuracy alone cannot guarantee beneficial outcomes. AI should support rather than replace human judgement. Responsible adoption therefore requires transparent governance, continuous monitoring, clear accountability and procedures that enable automated outputs to be independently questioned and verified.
